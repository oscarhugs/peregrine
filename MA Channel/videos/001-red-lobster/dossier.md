# Red Lobster: Dossier

## 1. One-line story

A century-old seafood chain survived a 2014 private-equity buyout that stripped its real estate and saddled it with above-market rent, then, a decade later, collapsed into Chapter 11 after a permanent all-you-can-eat shrimp promotion and a conflicted supplier-owner drained its remaining cash [S22][S25].

## 2. Cast

- **Red Lobster** — the restaurant chain, ~550 U.S. locations plus ~27 in Canada as of its 2024 filing [S20].
- **Darden Restaurants** — parent company (spun off from General Mills in 1995) that owned Red Lobster until 2014; sold it under shareholder pressure [S25][S05].
- **Starboard Value** — activist hedge fund that ran a 2014 proxy fight against Darden's board, arguing the Red Lobster sale destroyed shareholder value [S05].
- **Golden Gate Capital** — private-equity firm that bought Red Lobster from Darden in 2014 for ~$2.1B, then sold the real estate for $1.5B in a sale-leaseback [S05][S41].
- **American Realty Capital Properties (ARCP)** — REIT that bought Red Lobster's real estate from Golden Gate in 2014, becoming its landlord [S05].
- **Thai Union Group** — Thai seafood conglomerate and Red Lobster's largest shrimp supplier for 30+ years; bought a 49% stake in 2016 ($575M) and the remaining Golden Gate stake in 2020, becoming majority owner while still supplying it shrimp [S25][S24].
- **Paul Kenny** — Red Lobster's interim CEO from April 2022, installed "at the direction of Thai Union"; made Ultimate Endless Shrimp a permanent menu item in May 2023 [S25].
- **Jonathan Tibus** — restructuring executive (Alvarez & Marsal) retained January 2024, became CEO, signed the Chapter 11 first-day declaration [S25].
- **Fortress Credit Corp. / Fortress Investment Group** — administrative agent for Red Lobster's Prepetition Term Loan ($264.7M outstanding); became the stalking-horse buyer and post-bankruptcy owner [S25][S20].
- **Damola Adamolekun** — installed as CEO when Red Lobster exited bankruptcy in September 2024 [S23].

## 4. Deal mechanics

**The 2014 Darden → Golden Gate → ARCP transaction** (numbers here come from Starboard's 2014 proxy statement, a party to the fight rather than the SEC filings themselves — see Section 9):

- Darden sold Red Lobster's operating business *and* its owned real estate to Golden Gate Capital for approximately **$2.1 billion gross / $1.6 billion net** proceeds (after taxes) [S05].
- Golden Gate simultaneously signed an agreement to sell Red Lobster's real estate to American Realty Capital Properties (ARCP) for **$1.5 billion** [S05].
- Starboard's math: if $1.5B of the $1.6B net proceeds is attributable to real estate, the operating business itself fetched roughly **$100 million**, or under 1x EBITDA, before debt breakage and transaction costs — a figure Starboard called a "fire sale" [S05].
- The sale-leaseback converted Red Lobster from an owner-operator (no rent) into a tenant across most of its footprint, with rent contractually **escalating ~2%/year** [S41].
- By fiscal 2023, Red Lobster was paying **$190.5 million/year** in lease obligations, of which **$64 million** related specifically to underperforming stores [S25].
- The company leased 687 locations total: 247 under Master Leases, 440 individual property leases [S25]. In Canada specifically: 13 restaurants under a Master Lease (guaranteed by Red Lobster Intermediate Holdings), 13 under individual leases, plus 2 owned properties [S20].

**Ownership chain 2016–2024:** Thai Union bought a 49% stake for $575M in 2016, then acquired Golden Gate's remaining stake in 2020 alongside the "Seafood Alliance" investor group — putting Thai Union (47% common equity + 100% preferred) and Seafood Alliance (38% common equity) in control, with legacy management holding the remainder [S25].

**Prepetition capital structure (as of the May 2024 filing):**

| Facility | Lender/Agent | Amount outstanding |
|---|---|---|
| Prepetition Term Loan | Fortress Credit Corp. (agent) | ~$264.7 million |
| ABL Facility (letters of credit) | Wells Fargo (agent) | ~$29.3 million |
| **Total funded debt** | | **~$294 million** |

Source: [S25][S20]. Wells Fargo held a senior lien on current assets (cash, inventory, receivables); Fortress held a senior lien on all other assets [S25].

**The bankruptcy/sale structure:** Red Lobster entered into a Restructuring Support Agreement with the Term Loan Lenders (May 9, 2024), who agreed to serve as a "stalking horse" bidder via an entity they controlled, backstopped by a $100 million DIP financing commitment [S22][S25]. The RSA also triggered a parallel CCAA recognition proceeding in Ontario for the Canadian business (27 restaurants, ~4.7% of consolidated revenue, ~5.2% of the workforce) [S20]. The stalking horse bid was ultimately confirmed as the Successful Bidder, and Red Lobster emerged in September 2024 as an independent company with 545 locations and a new CEO [S21][S23].

## 5. What went wrong / right — weighing the causes

The video's central question — Endless Shrimp, the 2014 sale-leaseback, Thai Union's conflict of interest, or something else — can be weighed against the numbers in the record.

**The Endless Shrimp promotion.** In May 2023, then-CEO Paul Kenny made the historically limited-time Ultimate Endless Shrimp deal a permanent $20 menu item, over internal pushback [S25]. This is a **fact** from the CEO's own sworn declaration. It directly cost the company **$11 million** and created supply shortages so severe some restaurants went "days or weeks" without shrimp [S25]. (calc) $11M is roughly 5.8% of the $190.5M annual lease burden the company was carrying the same year ($11M / $190.5M ≈ 5.8%) — a real but comparatively small line item next to the rent bill. It is best understood as the proximate trigger, not the root cause: it hit a company that had already burned through $70M+ of cash reserves in six months for unrelated reasons [S25].

**The 2014 sale-leaseback.** This is the largest and longest-running cost identified in the sources. Rent obligations of $190.5M/year, escalating 2% annually, are an order of magnitude larger than the shrimp loss — Restaurant Dive and the American Prospect both describe this as the structural cause that "began" Red Lobster's decline a decade before the bankruptcy [S41][S44]. (calc) Over roughly ten years (2014–2024) with 2% annual escalation, cumulative extra rent burden compounds well into the billions if extrapolated from a ~$150M+ starting base — but the *exact* 2014 starting rent figure is not in the verified sources, so this projection should be treated as illustrative, not a sourced total. What is directly sourced is the $190.5M 2023 figure and the $64M of that tied to underperforming stores specifically [S25] — meaning over a third of the annual rent bill was going toward stores that were themselves not profitable, a structural mismatch between store performance and fixed lease cost.

**Thai Union's conflict of interest.** Thai Union was simultaneously Red Lobster's controlling shareholder (47% common equity + 100% preferred, per the debtor's own filing) and its primary shrimp supplier [S25]. The Tibus declaration states the company was "investigating" whether Thai Union and Kenny used the "quality review" pretext to eliminate two competing breaded-shrimp suppliers, leaving Thai Union as the exclusive, higher-cost supplier [S25]. By 2026, this had escalated from an internal investigation into an actual creditor lawsuit alleging breach of fiduciary duty and "uneconomic contracts" benefiting Thai Union at Red Lobster's expense [S27]. This is a **contested allegation**, not an adjudicated fact — Thai Union has called the claims "meritless" and filed its own counterclaim, alleging Red Lobster's abrupt demand-forecast changes left Thai Union holding $22.9M in excess inventory, with Red Lobster owing it $3.7M [S26]. Thai Union also took a $530 million non-cash impairment when it exited — losing nearly its entire original $575M investment — which is hard to square with a simple profit-extraction motive, though it does not rule out extraction having occurred earlier via the shrimp contracts specifically [S24].

**Verdict, weighing the evidence:** The sale-leaseback created the largest and most durable financial burden (structural, decade-long, ~$190M/year by 2023, disproportionately loaded onto weak stores) [S25][S41]. Endless Shrimp was a real but comparatively small ($11M) self-inflicted wound layered on top, at exactly the moment the company had no cash cushion left to absorb it [S25]. The Thai Union conflict-of-interest allegations, if proven, would explain *why* the Endless Shrimp mistake happened and persisted despite internal objections — but that causal link remains alleged and litigated, not established [S26][S27]. Golden Gate, for its part, is not shown in these sources to have made any public comment on the sale-leaseback's later consequences; its perspective is inferred only through the deal structure itself.

## 6. Myths vs. record

- **Myth: "$20 shrimp killed a 55-year-old company."** Record: the $11M shrimp-promotion loss was real but small next to the $190.5M annual lease burden the company was already carrying; multiple outlets and the CEO's own declaration frame the shrimp promotion as the final trigger on a company already structurally weakened [S25][S41][S26].
- **Myth: Red Lobster was simply a badly run restaurant chain.** Record: its own bankruptcy filing attributes much of the crisis specifically to "over-market and under-performing leases" inherited from the 2014 sale-leaseback, not merely operational execution [S20][S25].
- **Discrepancy worth flagging on-screen:** the American Prospect states Darden sold Red Lobster to Golden Gate for "$1.2 billion" [S44], while the Starboard proxy statement — filed contemporaneously by a party directly involved in fighting the deal — states $2.1 billion gross / $1.6 billion net [S05]. These sources conflict; the $2.1B/$1.6B figures should be treated as the better-sourced numbers pending confirmation against the original 2014 8-Ks (see Section 9), and the $1.2B figure should not be presented on screen without a caveat.

## 9. Open questions / legal care

- The 2014 Darden/ARCP/Golden Gate deal terms ($2.1B price, $1.5B real estate sale, specific lease terms) are currently sourced only to Starboard's 2014 proxy statement — an advocacy document from a party actively fighting Darden's board, not the primary SEC transaction filings. **Confirm against the original Darden and ARCP 2014 8-Ks** (identified in sources/INDEX.md as not yet available: Darden 8-Ks 2014-05-16, 2014-05-21, 2014-07-28, 8-K/A 2014-08-01; ARCP 8-K EX-99.1 2014-05-16 and 2014-07-28) before stating these numbers as flatly uncontested facts on screen.
- The American Prospect's $1.2 billion purchase-price figure [S44] directly conflicts with Starboard's $2.1B/$1.6B figures [S05] and has not been reconciled — flag as "alleged"/unresolved rather than picking one.
- All allegations of Thai Union "undue influence," coordinated elimination of competing suppliers, and profit-squeezing are **contested and currently in litigation** (a 2026 creditor-trust lawsuit); Thai Union disputes them and has filed its own counterclaim [S25][S26][S27]. On-screen language should say "alleged" throughout this thread, not "Thai Union did X."
- The exact number of restaurant closures during the Chapter 11 process, and the final sale/purchase price paid by Fortress-affiliated entities for the reorganized company, are not fully quantified in the currently verified sources (S20/S21 describe the process and confirm the Plan/Successful Bidder outcome, but the final consideration paid is not stated in the excerpts reviewed here) — worth a follow-up pass through S21 if a specific dollar figure is wanted for the exit.
- Golden Gate Capital's own perspective/public statements on the sale-leaseback and its long-term consequences do not appear directly in any of the currently verified sources; its role is described only through the deal mechanics and Starboard/journalistic commentary. If Golden Gate's voice is wanted for balance, it will need a new source.
