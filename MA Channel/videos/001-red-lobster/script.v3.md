## 00 Cold Open

In May 2024, Red Lobster filed for bankruptcy, and the internet blamed a twenty-dollar plate of endless shrimp. The shrimp cost eleven million dollars. That same year, Red Lobster's rent bill was about one hundred and ninety million. [S25] [VISUAL: number_counter $11M → $190.5M over a fast montage of Endless Shrimp ads and viral headlines]

Both numbers come from the same sworn statement, filed by the company's own restructuring chief. [S25] [VISUAL: doc_highlight S25 "costing the Debtors $11 million", then "$190.5 million in lease obligations"]

The shrimp story is easy to tell. It's also the least interesting part of what happened.

Before 2014, Red Lobster owned much of the real estate it operated on. [S41] So how did Red Lobster end up with a rent bill seventeen times bigger than its shrimp disaster? (calc: 190.5 / 11 ≈ 17)

The answer is a deal. It was signed in 2014, and it involved a restaurant company, a private equity firm, a real estate investment trust, and, later, the company that supplied the shrimp.

This is the postmortem of that deal.

## 01 The Golden Age

In 1968, a restaurateur named Bill Darden opened a seafood restaurant in Lakeland, Florida. [S25] He called it Red Lobster, and the idea was simple. Seafood, at a price a family could afford, in a place that wasn't a fancy coastal restaurant. [VISUAL: archival photo, first Red Lobster, Lakeland]

Two years later, General Mills bought it. [S25] Under the cereal giant, Red Lobster became a national chain, and in 1995 General Mills spun its restaurant business off as a separate public company, named after the founder. Darden Restaurants. [S25] [VISUAL: timeline 1968 → 1970 → 1995]

Red Lobster grew into the largest casual dining seafood chain in the United States. Even at the time of its bankruptcy, it served more than sixty-four million customers a year and bought twenty percent of all North American lobster tails. [S25] [VISUAL: stock footage, Red Lobster exteriors; number_counter 64M guests/yr]

Darden didn't just run Red Lobster restaurants. For a large share of them, it owned the land and the buildings too. No landlord. No rent check. [S41] That real estate sat quietly on the balance sheet, and for a long time nobody outside the company thought much about it.

In 2014, someone did.

## 02 The Pressure

By 2013, Red Lobster was in a slow decline. Casual dining was losing customers to fast-casual chains, and seafood costs were volatile. [S44] Darden's stock was lagging its peers.

That attracted an activist investor. In December 2013, the hedge fund Starboard Value disclosed a 5.6 percent stake in Darden and argued that the company was undervalued. [S05] [VISUAL: title card "Starboard Value · 5.6% · Dec 2013"]

Starboard's thesis was about real estate. Darden owned hundreds of restaurant properties, and Starboard believed that value could be unlocked for shareholders, ideally in a tax-efficient way, while Darden kept the upside of its restaurants.

Darden's board had a different plan. It wanted to separate Red Lobster from the rest of the company. Investors were split. A majority of Darden's shareholders formally asked the board to hold a special meeting before any sale. [S05]

The board didn't wait.

## 03 The Deal

On May 16, 2014, Darden announced it was selling Red Lobster, the restaurants and the real estate together, to the private equity firm Golden Gate Capital. The gross price was about two point one billion dollars. After taxes, Darden expected about one point six billion. [S05] [VISUAL: doc_highlight S05 "gross proceeds of approximately $2.1 billion and net proceeds of $1.6 billion"]

At the same moment, Golden Gate signed a second agreement. It would sell Red Lobster's real estate to a real estate investment trust called American Realty Capital Properties, for one point five billion dollars. [S05] [VISUAL: deal_flow Darden → Golden Gate $2.1B → ARCP $1.5B real estate]

This is called a sale-leaseback. A company sells the buildings it owns, then signs a long-term lease to keep operating in them. It gets a big check today. In exchange, it pays rent forever after.

Here is what that did to the math. [VISUAL: bar_compare: price paid $2.1B; real estate sold $1.5B; remainder $0.6B (calc)]

Golden Gate agreed to pay roughly two point one billion for Red Lobster. It agreed, at the same time, to sell the buildings for one point five billion. That leaves roughly six hundred million dollars for the restaurant business itself, before fees and financing. (calc) [S05]

Starboard ran the same numbers from Darden's side and was furious. In its proxy fight later that summer, it argued that Darden could have captured the real estate value itself, tax-free, and that the deal valued the actual restaurant business at about one hundred million dollars, less than one year of its earnings. Starboard called it a fire sale. [S05] [VISUAL: doc_highlight S05 "just $100 million in value for Red Lobster's operating business, or less than 1x EBITDA"]

> Legal care: the $100M figure is Starboard's argument, not an established value. Keep "Starboard argued".

For Red Lobster, the restaurant chain, the deal changed one thing permanently. A company that had not paid rent on those properties now had a landlord, and a rent bill scheduled to rise about two percent every year. [S41]

Why would a private equity firm structure a deal like this? The logic is well known in the industry. Selling the real estate reduces how much of the buyer's own money is tied up in the deal. The property investor gets a long, predictable stream of rent. And if the restaurants perform, everyone does well.

The risk sits with the tenant. Rent doesn't care whether customers show up. It escalates in good years and bad ones.

Starboard lost that battle but won the war. In October 2014, Darden's shareholders voted out all twelve directors and replaced them with Starboard's slate. [S55] But the Red Lobster sale had already closed. [VISUAL: title card "All 12 Darden directors replaced · Oct 10, 2014"]

## 04 The Turn

Golden Gate didn't hold Red Lobster alone for long. In 2016, Thai Union Group, a Thai seafood conglomerate and a large-scale supplier of shrimp to Red Lobster, invested five hundred seventy-five million dollars for a large minority stake. [S24] [S25]

In 2020, a group of investors led by Thai Union bought out Golden Gate's remaining stake. [S24] [S25] By the time of the bankruptcy filing, Thai Union held forty-seven percent of the common equity and all of the preferred equity in Red Lobster's parent. [S25] [VISUAL: ownership timeline chart 2014 → 2016 → 2020]

Red Lobster's controlling owner was also one of its most important suppliers. The same company that decided how much shrimp Red Lobster should buy was also the company selling it.

That arrangement isn't illegal, and it isn't unusual in food supply chains. But it creates a question every board has to manage. When the interests of the owner and the interests of the restaurant pull in different directions, which one wins?

Meanwhile, the restaurants were struggling. Red Lobster's guest count fell by about thirty percent between 2019 and 2023. [S25] The pandemic hit casual dining hard, and then inflation arrived. Menu prices at restaurants rose faster than grocery prices, and customers started eating at home. [S25] [VISUAL: line_reveal guest count index 2019–2023, −30%]

Leadership churned too. The chain's CEO resigned in April 2022, and, according to the company's later bankruptcy filing, Thai Union directed that one of its own executives, Paul Kenny, step in as acting interim CEO. [S25] [VISUAL: doc_highlight S25 footnote "At the direction of Thai Union, Mr. Kenny became acting interim CEO"]

And the rent kept rising.

## 05 The Collapse

In May 2023, Red Lobster made a decision that would become an internet punchline. Ultimate Endless Shrimp had always been a limited-time promotion. Paul Kenny made it a permanent twenty-dollar menu item. According to the company's own sworn filing, he did it despite significant pushback from other members of the management team. [S25] [VISUAL: stock/licensed Endless Shrimp promo imagery]

Customers loved it. That was the problem. People came in, ordered the deal, and kept ordering. The company says the promotion cost it eleven million dollars, and demand ran so far ahead of planning that some restaurants went days or weeks without certain types of shrimp. [S25]

The filing goes further. Red Lobster said it was investigating whether Thai Union and Kenny pushed the promotion unusually hard in stores, whether Kenny bypassed the normal supply planning process, and whether a so-called quality review was used to eliminate two competing shrimp suppliers, leaving Thai Union with an exclusive deal at higher cost. [S25] [VISUAL: doc_highlight S25 "under the guise of a 'quality review'"]

Those are allegations. Thai Union has called them meritless. [S41] It also filed a counterclaim, arguing that Red Lobster's own sudden forecast changes left Thai Union holding nearly twenty-three million dollars of excess inventory. [S26] In 2026, Red Lobster's creditors took the fight further and sued Thai Union, Kenny, and other former officers for breach of fiduciary duty. [S27] That case hasn't been decided.

Whatever happened inside those meetings, the cash was disappearing. In May 2023, Red Lobster had about one hundred million dollars in the bank. Over roughly six months, operating losses, a forced debt paydown, and thirty-two million dollars of annual interest drained it to less than thirty million. [S25] [VISUAL: line_reveal cash $100M → <$30M, May–Nov 2023]

By September 2023, the company was in default with its lenders and operating under a forbearance agreement. [S20] In January 2024, Thai Union announced it would exit its investment and write off five hundred thirty million dollars. Thai Union said owning Red Lobster no longer aligned with its capital allocation priorities. [S24]

In May 2024, Red Lobster abruptly closed roughly fifty restaurants. According to The American Prospect, employees found out when they showed up for work and saw the signs. [S44] On May 19, it filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in Florida. [S22] [VISUAL: courthouse exterior, M.D. Florida; doc_highlight S25 case caption]

## 06 The Verdict

So what actually killed Red Lobster?

Start with the shrimp, because that's what everyone remembers. Eleven million dollars is a real loss. But it is a small number next to the company's other costs. [S25]

Now look at the rent. In 2023, Red Lobster spent about one hundred ninety million dollars on its leases. That figure covers all six hundred eighty-seven leased locations, not only the properties sold in 2014, but the company's own filing singles out its leases as a central problem. A material portion, it says, were priced above market. And more than sixty-four million dollars of that rent went to restaurants that were underperforming. [S25] [VISUAL: bar_compare Endless Shrimp $11M vs rent on underperforming stores $64M vs total lease obligations $190.5M]

In other words, Red Lobster spent almost six times its entire shrimp loss, every year, just on rent for the restaurants that weren't pulling their weight. (calc: 64 / 11 ≈ 5.8)

That's the structural problem the 2014 deal created. Before the sale-leaseback, a weak restaurant on owned land could be closed, sold, or waited out. After it, the weak restaurant came with a lease, and the rent kept rising two percent a year whether the dining room was full or empty. [S41]

The industry saw it that way too. As one food service executive told Restaurant Dive, when that real estate deal took off, that was the beginning of the economics starting to go south. [S41]

But the lease alone doesn't explain the timing. Red Lobster carried that rent for nine years before it failed. What changed was everything around it: a thirty percent drop in guests, inflation, a revolving door of CEOs, an owner who was also a supplier, and a shrimp promotion that arrived exactly when the company had no cash cushion left. [S25]

So here is the fairest verdict the documents support. The 2014 sale-leaseback removed Red Lobster's margin for error, years before the company failed. The shrimp promotion was simply the mistake that finally arrived once that margin was gone.

In September 2024, Red Lobster emerged from bankruptcy, owned by the lenders who had financed it, led by Fortress Investment Group. [S23] It came out with about five hundred forty-five restaurants and a new CEO, Damola Adamolekun. [S23] It still leases its buildings.

## 07 The Lesson

Every deal has a price, and most people only look at the number on the front page. Two point one billion dollars. One point five billion for the real estate.

The more important price is the one that shows up every month for the next decade. When a company sells the ground it stands on, it swaps a one-time check for a permanent bill. Sometimes that's the right call. But it means the business has to win every year, because the rent will be there whether it does or not.

Red Lobster's rent was there every year. In 2023, the business stopped winning.
